How TriLens Times Works

Every story on TriLens Times gives you a 360-degree perspective: verified facts, the three dominant ways people see it, and prediction market probabilities. Here's how we build that view.

Why this exists

Most news shows you one perspective. Algorithms feed you what you already believe. Even "bias-aware" tools just label outlets as left, right, or center — which doesn't help you understand why thoughtful people disagree.

TriLens Times takes a different approach. For every major story, we identify the three most common ways people are interpreting it, then present each as its strongest version. The goal isn't to tell you what to think. It's to give you the complete picture so you can make better decisions.

Why three perspectives?

One perspective is bias. When you only hear one version of events, you have no way to evaluate it. You're not informed — you're captured.

Two is conflict. Binary framing forces every issue into for-or-against, creating false polarization. You pick a side instead of understanding the terrain.

Three is understanding. A third perspective breaks the binary and reveals the actual shape of a disagreement. You see where people genuinely differ — and where they quietly agree.

This follows Aristotle's classical pattern of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis. Three is the minimum number of data points needed to triangulate understanding — to move from "which side are you on?" to "what's actually happening?"

Three is also a cognitive sweet spot. Two creates tribalism. Five overwhelms. Three is enough to see the full picture without losing the thread.

What we do for every story

Verify the facts

Before anything else, we separate what is actually known from what is being claimed. Facts and perspectives are never blended.

Our fact-checking process

1

Multi-source cross-referencing

Every factual claim is checked against multiple independent sources. A fact is only marked "confirmed" when 3 or more credible sources agree on it.

2

Primary source priority

We prioritize wire services (AP, Reuters), official government data (BLS, Eurostat, central banks), and published research over commentary and opinion pieces.

3

Transparent confidence levels

Every fact is labeled: CONFIRMED (3+ sources agree), DISPUTED (sources disagree), or UNKNOWN (insufficient evidence). We never hide uncertainty.

4

Neutral language enforcement

Facts are stated without adjectives or framing. "Inflation rose 4.2%" — not "inflation surged" or "inflation crept up." We strip editorial spin from factual statements.

5

Source linking

Every confirmed fact links to its primary source so you can verify it yourself. We don't ask you to trust us — we give you the trail.

Our rule: We'd rather show fewer facts and be accurate than show more and be misleading. If we can't confirm it, we say so.

Show the 360° perspective

We analyze coverage across dozens of sources to identify the three most distinct ways people are interpreting the same facts. Then we present each one as its strongest version — the way its most thoughtful proponents would make the case.

For each perspective, we include:

  • • Core Thesis — The central argument
  • • Why People Believe It — The reasoning, stated fairly
  • • Evidence Cited — The specific data and events supporters point to
  • • What Would Weaken It — What would have to be true for this view to be wrong

Why we don't use "Left / Right / Center"

Political labels flatten complex arguments into tribal identities. A debate about inflation might feature "structural failure vs. temporary shock vs. measurement error" — none of which map neatly to partisan categories. We label perspectives by what they actually argue, not which team they belong to.

"What Would Weaken It" is the most important section. It forces every perspective to state its own falsification conditions. This builds intellectual humility by design — you see that even the perspective you agree with has conditions under which it would be wrong.

Add prediction market probabilities

For each story, we show what prediction markets (Polymarket, Kalshi) say about related outcomes. These are probabilities backed by real money — traders who are wrong lose money, so there's a built-in incentive for accuracy.

We display the probability, trading volume (so you can judge how reliable the signal is), and 7-day trend. Markets are imperfect, but they add an accountability layer that opinion columns don't have.

Our information hierarchy

Every TriLens Times story follows the same structure. This is a hard rule, never violated:

1

Facts first

What is confirmed, disputed, and unknown

2

Perspectives second

The three dominant ways people see it, steelmanned

3

Synthesis third

Common ground, core disagreement, key signal, and decision-relevant takeaway

4

Probabilities fourth

What markets forecast, with volume and trend

5

Manipulation warnings last

Loaded language, missing context, and cherry-picked data

These categories are always visually separated and never blended. This is the core design principle of TriLens Times.

What we don't do

×

We don't tell you which perspective is correct

×

We don't editorially choose which stories appear — selection is based on coverage volume across sources

×

We don't force three perspectives when only two exist — if the genuine disagreement is between two views, we show two

×

We don't use "left/right/center" labels — arguments are described by their content, not their tribe

×

We don't present prediction market odds as truth — they're trader consensus, which is informative but fallible

Corrections

Every story has a "Suggest a correction" link. We review all submissions and update stories when errors are found. We publish a monthly transparency report covering:

  • • Number of corrections received and implemented
  • • Categories of errors (factual, perspective labeling, source attribution)
  • • Stories where perspective labels were revised

AI-generated content can contain errors. We encourage you to follow source links and verify claims independently.
